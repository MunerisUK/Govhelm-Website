Platform

One core, many governments

Eight reference jurisdictions are committed to the platform today, spanning Crown Dependencies, Overseas Territories, an EU republic and two Caribbean states. They run the same image. Everything that differs between them is a value in a profile.

Variation, in practice

What each profile proves

A profile is only interesting if it forces the architecture to bend somewhere. Each of these bends it in a different direction.

Crown Dependency

Government of Jersey

Anchor tenant. Income tax and GST on; parish administration and residential qualification both enabled.

Crown Dependency

States of Guernsey

A neighbouring Bailiwick with a different assembly, a different regulator and its own parish structure.

Crown Dependency

Isle of Man Government

A bicameral legislature, which the profile expresses as a value rather than a variant build.

Overseas Territory

Government of Gibraltar

A local currency at par with sterling, and a data-protection regime aligned to UK GDPR rather than EU GDPR.

Sovereign state

Government of Malta

A republic, in the eurozone, with data that must reside in the EU. Three variation points that would be three forks in a bespoke build.

Overseas Territory

Cayman Islands Government

No personal income tax: revenue_taxation is toggled off and customs and duties carry the revenue function instead.

Sovereign state

Government of Barbados

A parliamentary republic since 2021 — the head-of-state change is a profile edit, not a release.

Sovereign state

Government of Jamaica

Two orders of magnitude larger than the Crown Dependencies, on the same core and the same schema.

The schema

Every variation point a profile must resolve

A profile is validated against a list of required paths before a tenant is served. Miss one and the platform refuses to start that tenant and names the missing field. There is no default for a constitutional fact.

Beyond the required set, a profile carries the optional detail a jurisdiction needs: its departments and which domain each owns, its identity schemes and issuers, its fiscal design, its data-sharing agreements, its integration endpoints and its extension configuration.

  • jurisdiction.name, .status and .domain
  • constitution.head_of_state.form — monarchy or republic
  • constitution.legislature.chambers — unicameral, bicameral or sui generis
  • constitution.legal_system.base
  • data_protection.regime
  • localisation.languages and the fiscal currency code
  • branding.logo — the government’s own mark, never ours
  • capabilities — the toggle block
  • residency.cross_tenant_access

config/jurisdiction-profiles/<tenant>.yaml (excerpt)

jurisdiction:
  name: "Government of Malta"
  status: sovereign_state
  iso_country: "MT"
  domain: "gov.mt"

constitution:
  head_of_state:
    form: republic              # differs from every Crown Dependency
  legislature:
    chambers: unicameral
  legal_system:
    base: civil_law

fiscal:
  currency:
    code: "EUR"                 # eurozone

data_protection:
  regime: "EU GDPR + Maltese Data Protection Act"
  gdpr_alignment: member_state

residency:
  data_region: "eu"             # must reside in the EU
  customer_managed_keys: true
  cross_tenant_access: forbidden

Onboarding a government

  1. 1

    Write the profile

    Copy the nearest reference profile and replace the values: constitution, departments, identity schemes, fiscal design, data-protection regime, languages, residency.

  2. 2

    Drop in the official logo

    The government’s own mark goes in the brand directory and the profile points at it. A tenant portal carries its government’s identity, never ours.

  3. 3

    Declare the agreements and toggles

    Either inherit the platform baseline register or declare the jurisdiction’s own, and switch on the capabilities it runs.

  4. 4

    Deploy the tenancy

    Add the tenant descriptor. One isolated release, its own database, its own region, its own host. That is the entire onboarding.

What never varies

The parts no profile may switch off

Configurability has to stop somewhere, and where it stops is a product decision worth stating plainly. A jurisdiction can turn off a capability, change its constitutional facts, declare its own agreements and choose its region.

It cannot turn off the policy check, opt out of the audit log, permit cross-tenant access, or ask for raw data where the architecture returns a claim. Those are not features with toggles; they are the reason the platform can be trusted with a citizen record at all.

Every committed profile sets cross_tenant_access: forbidden, and a test asserts that every tenant does. It is checked rather than assumed.

The guarantees in detail

Adding a jurisdiction should be boring

If onboarding a government is exciting, something is wrong with the architecture. The measure of this design is that the ninth profile is as dull to write as the eighth, and that nobody has to open the core to add it.
