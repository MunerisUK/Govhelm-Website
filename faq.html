Resources

Questions, answered plainly

The questions that come up in evaluation, including the ones where the honest answer is inconvenient for us. If yours is not here, ask it — and the answer will end up on this page.

Commercial

Licensing, cost and contract

How is GovHelm priced?

A per-tenant platform subscription banded by population, plus capability modules, plus a transparent implementation and connector quote. Per-seat pricing was designed for enterprise software and makes no sense for services that every resident of a jurisdiction uses.

No price is published on this site. Whether to publish one is a commercial decision that has not been taken; a figure is given on the first call rather than after three meetings.

Is it open source?

No. GovHelm is commercial software under a per-tenant subscription. The architecture pack and the design are published so that they can be reviewed before anything is procured, and source code review under a non-disclosure agreement is available during evaluation.

What happens if we want to leave?

You get documented exports in documented formats, and your jurisdiction profile is yours — it is your government’s configuration, not our intellectual property. Exit terms belong in the contract rather than in a negotiation held at the worst possible moment.

How much of the total cost is implementation rather than licence?

In government programmes generally, integration services dwarf licence cost — commonly by three or four to one. Budget on that basis and be suspicious of anyone who implies otherwise, including us. The difference here is that the integration work is connectors and a profile rather than a bespoke build, so it is smaller and it is reusable.

Are statutory or regulatory changes charged as change requests?

Changes a jurisdiction cannot decline should not be quoted as optional work. The things governments change most often — agreements, toggles, constitutional facts, branding — are profile configuration you control rather than chargeable work. Where a legislative change needs platform work, it is delivered under the subscription.

Technical

Fit, integration and operations

Do we have to replace our ERP, HCM or analytics platform?

No, and you should not want to. GovHelm brokers them across a governed seam: minimised claims in, instructions out, every field policy-gated and audited. Finance stays in the ERP, payroll in the HCM, clinical data in the care system, analytics in your own lakehouse.

What if our jurisdiction needs something the profile cannot express?

Then the schema gains a variation point, available to every tenant, rather than the code gaining a branch. That is the rule that keeps one image serving every government, and it is enforced by a lint and a test on every commit rather than by good intentions.

Can we run it in our own cloud, or on our own hardware?

Yes. The deployment is a container, a tenant-agnostic chart and a Postgres database, placed in your declared region with your own key custody. There is nothing in the design that requires a particular hyperscaler.

Is there an API?

There is a working REST surface today, documented on the API reference page, together with per-tenant open-data endpoints. It is not yet versioned and carries no stability commitment; a versioned, supported API with a deprecation policy arrives with 1.0.

What does it need to run?

A container runtime, an ingress, and Postgres. Resource requests are modest and production descriptors run two to three replicas. For most jurisdictions the binding constraint is data residency and key custody, not capacity.

Governance

Data protection, audit and sovereignty

How is this compatible with our data-protection regime?

The regime is profile data, and eight are carried today across GDPR member states, adequacy-aligned local laws and Caribbean data-protection acts. The mechanism is the same everywhere: no access without an active agreement naming consumer, domain, purpose and permitted fields, with the legal basis recorded and every decision logged.

The design is written to be read by a data-protection authority rather than around one. We would rather have that conversation in month one than in month twelve.

Can a citizen see who looked at their data?

Yes, including what was refused. Allows and denies both land in an immutable per-tenant audit log with a citizen-facing dashboard onto it. Refusals are the entries an oversight body most wants and that most systems discard.

Could data reach another government’s tenancy?

Not by design and not by configuration. Each government runs its own deployment against its own database in its own region, cross-tenant access is forbidden in every profile, and a test asserts that across every committed tenant. An agreement in one tenancy cannot authorise an access in another.

Who can compel disclosure of our citizens’ data?

Whoever has jurisdiction over where it sits and who holds the keys — which is why both are profile properties rather than contractual assurances. If sovereignty is a binding constraint for you, it is the first thing to put on the table, not the last.

Has the governance design been independently assured?

Not yet. It is scheduled ahead of the first production tenancy and it is the first item on the roadmap, because for a platform whose proposition is trust it matters more than any capability. Until it has happened, the site will not imply that it has.

Risk

The questions a business case has to answer

You have no production deployments. Why would we go first?

Because the alternative is a programme with a well-documented overrun pattern, and because going first here is smaller than going first anywhere else: one capability, a profile, a connector, and an evaluation environment you can falsify the claims in before committing.

That said, if proven production scale is your binding criterion, it is a real reason to choose something else today, and the comparison page says so before it says anything else.

What happens to us if Muneris disappears?

It is a fair question to ask a young supplier and a bad one to leave unasked. The mitigations are documented exports in documented formats, source code review under non-disclosure during evaluation, and source code escrow. None of that makes supplier concentration a non-issue; it makes it a survivable one.

How do we de-risk the first phase?

Land one capability rather than a programme. Life events and Tell Us Once is the usual choice because the governance story becomes tangible the moment somebody opens the audit dashboard. Prove it, then open the toggles.

What is the honest worst case?

That the connector work into a genuinely legacy estate is harder and slower than the seam makes it look. Integration depth is where these programmes actually get difficult, and no architecture diagram removes that. Budget for it, stage it, and make the first phase small enough that discovering it is affordable.
