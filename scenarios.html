Deployment scenarios

Four illustrative scenarios, written from the reference profiles the platform already carries. They show how the same image meets four very different constitutions — and where each one puts pressure on the design.

These are illustrative, not case studies

No government is running GovHelm in production. The scenarios below are composites built from the committed reference profiles and from published transformation plans — not accounts of work delivered for a named organisation.

They are here because a buyer needs to see how the architecture behaves against a real constitution, not because we are implying customers we do not have. When there are case studies, they will replace this page and they will be named.

Scenario one · Crown Dependency

Land Tell Us Once first, then open the toggles

A Crown Dependency of around a hundred thousand people, nine consolidated departments, a parish tier with its own civic functions, and a residency regime that gates housing and work rights. An ERP programme already delivered at considerable cost, and no appetite for another.

The shape of it. Keep the ERP. Deploy the governed layer over the existing registries, with identity and civil registration as the authoritative source and one capability switched on: life events. Registering a death once ceases the pension, clears the electoral roll and — where payroll is connected — forwards a leaver instruction, each fan-out checked against its own agreement.

Where it presses on the design. Two jurisdiction-specific functions, parish administration and residential qualification control, exist here and almost nowhere else. Both ship in the image every government runs and switch on by toggle, with the parish count and the status categories carried as profile data. That is the fork that does not happen.

Profile at a glance

  • Crown Dependency, monarchy, unicameral assembly
  • Sterling, with a local issue at par
  • Income tax and a goods and services tax, both enabled
  • Both country-specific extensions switched on
  • Data held in-jurisdiction or an approved region, own keys

First ninety days

  1. 1

    Write the profile

    Constitution, departments, identity schemes, agreements.

  2. 2

    Connect one source

    Civil registration as the authoritative source for life events.

  3. 3

    Run the fan-out

    Pension and electoral services subscribe; the audit log fills.

  4. 4

    Show the citizen

    The audit dashboard is the thing that convinces people.

Profile at a glance

  • Overseas Territory, monarchy, local dollar
  • revenue_taxation: false — no personal income tax
  • indirect_revenue_customs: true — duties carry the revenue function
  • Local data-protection act, in-territory residency

The toggle that would be a fork elsewhere

A platform that assumes personal income tax needs a variant build for a jurisdiction without one. Here it is one line in a profile, and a test asserts that it stays a toggle.

Scenario two · Overseas Territory

A government with no income tax at all

An Overseas Territory of eighty-odd thousand people, its own currency pegged to the dollar, a financial-services economy, and no personal income tax. Revenue comes from customs, duties and fees, so the capability a mature govtech suite assumes is central here does not exist at all.

The shape of it. The revenue capability is switched off and customs and indirect revenue is switched on. The benefits means test no longer reads a revenue claim, because there is no revenue domain to read — it reads what the jurisdiction’s own agreements authorise instead. Nothing about the core changes.

Where it presses on the design. This is the scenario that tests whether “configuration not code” is true or is marketing. If a capability being absent breaks a service that assumed it, the product-line claim collapses. So it is asserted by test, on every commit, against the committed profile.

Scenario three · EU island republic

A republic, the euro, and data that must stay in the EU

A sovereign island state of half a million people inside the European Union. A republic rather than a monarchy, the euro rather than sterling, EU GDPR rather than an adequacy-aligned local law, and a hard requirement that citizen data resides in the EU.

The shape of it. Three variation points that would be three forks in a bespoke build are three values in a profile: the head-of-state form, the currency code, and the residency region. The deployment is scheduled into an EU region with its own database and its own keys, and the regime the agreements cite is the Maltese implementation of GDPR rather than the platform baseline.

Where it presses on the design. Data residency has to be a first-class property rather than an operational promise. It is part of the profile schema, it drives placement, and cross-tenant access is forbidden in the profile — so the question “could this data reach another tenancy?” has a structural answer rather than a procedural one.

Why this one matters commercially

  • EU interoperability obligations raise the bar regionally
  • Sovereignty questions disqualify some suppliers outright
  • A republic profile proves the schema is not Westminster-only
  • Euro pricing and EU residency are procurement gating items

The platform renders Westminster-derived governments particularly well because that is what the architecture pack studied. This scenario is the check that it is not only that.

What changes at scale

  • Replica counts and database sizing in the tenant descriptor
  • The identity resolver carries far more aliases per person
  • Audit volume becomes an operational design question
  • Nothing in the core, the schema or the capability catalogue

Scenario four · Larger Commonwealth realm

Two orders of magnitude larger, same architecture

A Commonwealth realm of nearly three million people, a freely floating currency, a 2020 data-protection act and a modern regulator. Thirty times the population of the anchor jurisdiction.

The shape of it. The same image, the same schema, the same capability catalogue, a different descriptor. Scale changes the sizing and the operational design of the audit store; it does not change the architecture, because nothing in the architecture assumed a small population.

Where it presses on the design. Honestly: this is the scenario furthest from anything that has been load-tested, and the site is not going to pretend otherwise. A jurisdiction at this scale should expect to see a performance programme in the delivery plan and an assurance gate before go-live, and to ask hard questions about audit retention.

Across all four

What every scenario has in common

Four constitutions, four currencies, four regulators, four capability sets — and one image, one schema and one set of guarantees.

One image

No jurisdiction runs a fork. A fix or a capability lands everywhere at once, behind a toggle.

One tenancy each

Its own deployment, database, region and keys, with cross-tenant access forbidden in the profile.

One audit trail

Every access, allow or deny, readable by the citizen it concerns, under the regime that jurisdiction is governed by.
